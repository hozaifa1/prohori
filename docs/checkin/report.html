What Prohori does. You tape a printed ChArUco board next to a concrete crack and take a phone photo. OpenCV finds the board, corrects for camera angle and scale, and reports the crack width in millimetres with an error range. If the photo can't support a trustworthy number (too far, blurred, tilted, glare), Prohori says so and tells the user exactly how to retake it. Crack width is what engineers use to decide whether concrete needs repair, and today it is mostly judged by eye with a plastic crack card.

1. Summary

  • Done: the full measurement pipeline (ChArUco detection, pose, rectification, per-crack width and uncertainty), a statistically calibrated quality gate, an agent that decides the next photo, a phone web app, and a production AWS deployment with storage, alerts, privacy redaction and cost guards.
  • Tested: 29 real phone photos of a printed test panel with 8 cracks of known width (0.30 to 3.00 mm), 18,683 measurements on degraded versions of those photos, a pre-registered agent study, and a public benchmark of 19,098 real crack profiles (krkCMd).
  • AWS spend so far: almost nothing, by design. Everything has run inside the account's Free Tier credits; the full agent evaluation on Amazon Bedrock cost $0.061. The grant money is set aside for the GPU, Graviton4 and hosting work in the final stretch (section 5).
  • Left before 26 October: deploy the calibrated gate, train a learned crack finder on public datasets, run the Graviton4 + OpenCV COOL benchmark, validate on real walls, then the write-up and video (section 6).

3. Results so far

All numbers come from files in our repository (eval/results/). We report held-out ("out-of-sample") numbers where we have them, and say where we don't.

Width accuracy on real phone photos

29 photos of the printed test panel; the board was found in 23 and 168 crack widths were measured.

SetCracksAvg. error, tuned on all photosAvg. error, each photo held out
Cracks the gate certifies850.045 mm0.048 mm
All measured cracks1680.101 mm0.104 mm
Uncertified cracks830.159 mmn/a

Certified cracks are about 3.5 times more accurate than uncertified ones (3.6% vs 18.3% relative error), which is what the gate is for.

Can you trust the certificate?

  • Calibrated on 18,683 measurements from 2,584 degraded captures (blur, distance, tilt, glare, JPEG).
  • Held-out wrong-and-certified rate: 4.4% against a 5% target ("wrong" = off by more than 0.05 mm or 10%).
  • Holding out each real photo in turn: 53% of cracks certified, and 7.9% of certified cracks were wrong. A stricter setting gets this to 3.8%.
  • The old hand-set gate certified only 15% of cracks and 29% of those were wrong. On the same held-out data the calibrated gate certifies 2.5 times as many (38.5%) and cuts the wrong share to 12%.
  • The stated 90% range held the true width 92.9% of the time on held-out photos. The old range covered only 63% at 2 mm.

Agent vs fixed rules (pre-registered)

We wrote the test plan and pass bar before running it, then tested on 100 held-out simulated capture problems. Success = a usable photo within 3 actions.

Problem typeRandomFixed rulesBedrock agent (Nova Lite)
One thing wrong (20 cases)30%95%100%
Several things wrong at once (48 cases)2%62%85%

What we learned, honestly: a carefully hand-written rule reached 88% on the same cases, so the agent's real edge over a well-tuned rule set is small. We will publish that result as it is. It is also why the free rules policy is the default and the AI agent is an option.

Public benchmark (krkCMd, 19,098 real crack profiles)

This benchmark showed how image resolution limits accuracy: cracks of 0.30 to 0.50 mm need about 20 px/mm for errors near 0.05 mm, and our width formula was not yet good enough at low resolution. A simpler estimator reached 13 µm average error at full resolution. These findings directly changed the gate's resolution rules, and the estimator rework is on the remaining-work list.

5. Credits and grant: what we have spent and why

Short answer: our AWS costs so far are close to zero, on purpose. We built the whole stack to run cheaply first, so the grant can pay for the expensive parts at the end (GPU training, the Graviton4 benchmark, and keeping the service online for judging).
